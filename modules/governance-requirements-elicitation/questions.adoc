== Name the four elicitation techniques, what each delivers and what it costs

covers: lo-1

.Answer
[%collapsible]
====
[cols="1,2,1,1",options="header"]
|===
|Technique |Delivers |Cost |Blind spot

|*Interview*
|reasons, exceptions, what people want — the "why"
|1 hour per person, plus preparation and write-up
|people describe the process as it *should* be

|*Fragebogen*
|numbers over many people: how often, how many, how important
|days of waiting; a low response rate
|no follow-up question is possible

|*Beobachtung*
|what people actually do, including unmentioned workarounds
|half a day per workplace
|being watched changes the behaviour

|*Dokumentenanalyse*
|fields, data types, value ranges, vocabulary, volumes, rules
|hours, and nobody else's time
|shows the rule, never the exception
|===

The blind-spot column is the reason nobody uses one technique alone: each is
blind exactly where another one sees. An interview cannot tell you what people do
without noticing they do it; observation cannot tell you why; a document cannot
be asked anything at all; a questionnaire cannot follow up.

They also differ in what kind of statement they produce. Interview and
observation are *qualitative* — they find out what is going on. The questionnaire
is *quantitative* — it says how widespread it is. Document analysis is the only
one that yields *structure*: field names, types, ranges.

Points the answer must contain:

* Interview, Fragebogen, Beobachtung, Dokumentenanalyse
* What each delivers: reasons and exceptions; numbers over many people; actual
  behaviour; fields, vocabulary and volumes
* Each has a blind spot the others cover; nobody uses one alone
====

== How do you prepare and conduct an interview so the answers are usable?

covers: lo-2

.Answer
[%collapsible]
====
*Before:* read the documents first, write down what you want to know at the end
of the hour, turn that into six to ten questions, and decide who has to be in the
room. An hour is the maximum that stays productive.

*During:* ask *open* questions and follow what turns up. The semi-structured form
— prepared questions, free follow-ups — is the normal case.

[cols="1,1",options="header"]
|===
|Weak |Better

|"So you need an export function, right?"
|"What do you do with the list once training is over?"

|"Is the current process annoying?"
|"Walk me through the last time you recorded attendance."

|"Should the system be fast?"
|"How long may it take before it disturbs your work?"
|===

The left column is *suggestive*: it offers the answer, and a polite person agrees.
What you take home is then your own idea with somebody else's name on it — which
is worse than having no answer, because it looks like evidence.

*Afterwards:* write the protocol the same day — after two days you remember your
interpretation rather than their sentences — and send it back for confirmation.
The corrections that come back are usually the most valuable sentences of the
whole exercise, because they are the points where you misunderstood without
noticing.

A recording needs consent, and personal data from the interview stays out of the
repository and out of any prompt.

Points the answer must contain:

* Prepare: read the documents, write down the goal, six to ten questions, one hour
* Open instead of suggestive questions; semi-structured is the normal form
* Protocol the same day, sent back for confirmation
====

== What does a document analysis deliver that no interview can, and how do you run one?

covers: lo-3

.Answer
[%collapsible]
====
It delivers *structure*, and it delivers it before you have spoken to anybody:
field names, data types, value ranges, mandatory fields, volumes, the official
vocabulary, and the rules that are baked into an Excel formula which nobody in
the organisation can state out loud any more.

[cols="1,2",options="header"]
|===
|Source |What you get out of it

|forms, printed lists, sign-in sheets
|the fields, their order, which are mandatory, what a signature means

|Excel sheets and their formulas
|the actual rules

|exports, database dumps, log files
|data types, value ranges, volumes

|regulations, statutes, house rules, law
|constraints that are not negotiable

|manuals and old documentation
|the official vocabulary, and where it has drifted
|===

The procedure: *collect and catalogue* (what exists, who owns it, from when, still
in use?); *extract the fields* — every column is a candidate attribute with name,
type, range and example values, which is the first free draft of your data model;
*build the glossary* in the client's words, because those are the words the
acceptance will use; *note the contradictions* — a column that is always empty,
two forms with different fields for the same thing — as questions for the
interview; and *record what stayed unclear* as an assumption with its source.

Two further advantages are structural. It costs nobody else's time, so it can be
done before any appointment exists. And it has no Hawthorne effect: a form does
not behave differently because you are reading it.

Its limit is equally clear: a document shows the rule, never the exception
somebody makes every Friday, and it cannot be asked *why*. That is precisely the
division of labour with the interview — which is why the analysis comes first and
supplies the questions.

Use anonymised copies or the structure only; real documents carry personal data
that you do not need and should not hold.

Points the answer must contain:

* Fields, data types, value ranges, vocabulary, volumes, rules — structure
* Collect and catalogue, extract fields, build a glossary, note contradictions as
  interview questions, record assumptions
* It costs nobody else's time and has no observer effect; but it cannot be asked
  why, and it hides the exceptions
====

== In which order do you use the techniques, and why that order?

covers: lo-4

.Answer
[%collapsible]
====
.Documents first, then people, then a check for breadth
[graphviz,q-elicitation-order,svg]
----
digraph order {
  rankdir=LR;
  bgcolor="transparent";
  node [shape=box, style=filled, fillcolor="#f2f2f2", color="#666666",
        fontname="Helvetica", fontsize=11];
  edge [color="#666666", arrowsize=0.7, fontname="Helvetica", fontsize=10];

  D [label="Dokumentenanalyse\nfields, vocabulary,\nvolumes"];
  I [label="Interview\nthe why, the exceptions,\nthe contradictions"];
  B [label="Beobachtung\nwhat really happens\nat the desk"];
  F [label="Fragebogen\nhow often, how many,\nhow important"];

  D -> I [label="better questions"];
  I -> B [label="check the claims"];
  B -> F [label="quantify what you found"];
}
----

*Document analysis first*, because it is the only one that costs nobody else
anything, and because it makes every later question better. Walking into an
interview already knowing the fields, the vocabulary and the volumes means the
hour is spent on what only a person can answer.

*Interview second*, for the reasons, the exceptions and the contradictions the
documents produced.

*Observation* where the description and the reality are likely to differ — that
is, wherever people have worked around the official process.

*Questionnaire last*, to find out how widespread what you found actually is.
First it would ask the wrong questions very efficiently.

The order is a default, not a law. If there are no documents — a process that
exists only in people's heads — you start with an interview and build the field
list from it. What stays fixed is the reasoning: use the cheapest technique that
can still answer the question you have now.

Points the answer must contain:

* Documents, then interview, then observation, then questionnaire
* Document analysis costs nobody else's time and makes the later questions better
* The questionnaire needs interviews first, otherwise it asks the wrong things
====

== Two techniques for two situations, each with a justification

covers: lo-4

.Answer
[%collapsible]
====
*"The trainers say the paper list works fine, but three of them keep a private
Excel sheet."* — Beobachtung. The statement and the behaviour disagree, and only
watching resolves that. An interview will get the description again, because
people do not experience their workarounds as part of the process. Stay long
enough for the first twenty minutes of best behaviour to wear off.

*"We need to know how many of the 400 members would use online booking, and how
often."* — Fragebogen. It is a question about quantity across a population that
is far too big to interview, the answers are closed (yes/no, a number, a scale),
and no follow-up is needed. Pretest it with three people first.

The justification always has the same shape: name what you need to know, then
name the technique whose *output* is that kind of statement — reasons, numbers,
behaviour, or structure — and check that its blind spot is not exactly your
question.

Points the answer must contain:

* Match the technique to the kind of statement needed: reasons, numbers,
  behaviour, structure
* Contradiction between description and reality -> observation
* A question about how many or how often across many people -> questionnaire
====

== What must elicitation produce before it counts as finished?

covers: lo-5

.Answer
[%collapsible]
====
Three artefacts, all of them in the repository:

* *Verifiable goals* for the charter — an observable action, a measurable
  condition, a context. Notes are not requirements until somebody who was not
  there can check them.
* A *glossary* in the client's vocabulary. If the sign-in sheet says *Einheit*
  and your specs say session, the two documents will describe different things by
  March, and the acceptance will be about vocabulary rather than about the
  system.
* A list of *open questions and assumptions*, each with its source: which document
  or which conversation it came from.

The third is the one teams skip and the cheapest insurance in the project. An
assumption that is written down can be corrected in a five-minute conversation
("we assumed a member belongs to exactly one group — is that true?"). The same
assumption held silently is discovered at the acceptance, when it has a data model
built on top of it.

The check that elicitation is actually done: every goal in the charter can be
traced back to something somebody said, did, or wrote down — and nothing in it
came from the team's imagination alone.

Points the answer must contain:

* Verifiable goals, a glossary in the client's words, a list of open questions and
  assumptions with their sources
* An assumption written down is cheap to correct; one left in somebody's head is
  found at the acceptance
* Every goal must be traceable back to a source outside the team
====
