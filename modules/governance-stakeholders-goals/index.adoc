== Learning outcomes

* [[lo-1]] Say what a stakeholder is, give examples, tell internal from external
  ones, and explain that each of them pursues interests of their own
* [[lo-2]] Turn a wish into a goal that can be verified by someone else
* [[lo-3]] Distinguish goals from non-goals and explain why non-goals are written
  down
* [[lo-4]] Name the three dimensions a project goal is measured in, and tell
  outcome goals from process goals
* [[lo-5]] Agree a goal using the four questions: what for, for whom, end
  result, success criteria
* [[lo-6]] Recognise conflicting, complementary and independent goals, and
  weight sub-goals instead of pretending they are equal
* [[lo-7]] Explain how a goal hierarchy operationalises the overall goal, and
  build one top-down, bottom-up or with the mixed approach

== Stakeholders

A *stakeholder* is a person, a group or an organisation that is affected by the
project, can affect it, or believes itself affected by it — the definition of
ÖNORM ISO 21500. The last third matters: someone who wrongly believes they are
affected still acts, and still costs you a meeting.

Every stakeholder pursues *interests of their own*, and those are not yours.
They are usually legitimate, they routinely conflict with each other, and none
of them is written on the ticket. That is the whole reason for listing them: a
project fails at acceptance because somebody's interest was never asked about,
not because somebody was unknown.

[cols="1,2,2",options="header"]
|===
| |*Internal stakeholders* |*External stakeholders*

|Who
|inside the organisation running the project: client, project management, the
 team, the departments whose work changes
|outside it: users, customers, suppliers, authorities, data protection, the
 public

|In a school project
|the team, the teacher supervising it, the school's IT operations
|the club or company the project is for, its members, the parents, whoever hosts
 the server afterwards

|Typical interest
|a result that is defensible, on time, with no extra work landing on them
|a system that helps them rather than one that is merely delivered
|===

.Examples and what the interest behind them is
[cols="2,3",options="header"]
|===
|Stakeholder |Their interest

|the trainer who records attendance
|to be finished faster than with paper — otherwise they simply will not use it

|the club's committee
|to be able to show where the membership fees went

|members whose data is stored
|that as little as possible about them is stored, and that they can have it
 deleted

|whoever runs the server afterwards
|not to be woken up by it, and not to be left with something they cannot patch
|===

== Goals that can be verified

A goal is verifiable when somebody who was not involved can check it without
asking you what you meant.

[cols="1,1",options="header"]
|===
|Wish |Verifiable goal

|"The app should be fast."
|"Opening the attendance list for a group of 30 takes under one second on the
 lab laptops."

|"Users should like it."
|"A trainer can record a full session without written instructions, in under two
 minutes."

|"The data must be safe."
|"Personal data is stored only for members with a signed consent, and can be
 deleted on request within one working day."
|===

The pattern: an observable action, a measurable condition, a context in which it
holds. If your goal has none of those, it is a wish — write it down as one, but
do not call it a goal.

Two properties make a goal a good one: it is *achievable*, and it is
*quantifiable* — whether it was reached can be measured. And it prescribes no
solution: "trainers record attendance digitally" is a goal, "we build a React
app" is a decision that comes later.

=== Scope, schedule, cost

Project goals are stated in three dimensions, and a goal that names only one of
them is not yet agreed.

----

----

They are coupled: hold two of them and the third follows. In a school project
the *schedule* (Termine) is fixed by the calendar and the *cost* (Kosten) is
fixed by the hours you actually have, which leaves exactly one adjustable
dimension — *scope and quality* (Leistung). A team that refuses to cut scope ends
up cutting quality instead, silently.

This dimension has two halves and both belong in the goal: the *scope* (which
functions) and the *quality* (how well they have to work — the non-functional
side, see `sdd-why-specs`).

=== Outcome goals and process goals

[cols="1,2,2",options="header"]
|===
| |*Outcome goal* (Ergebnisziel) |*Process goal* (Vorgehensziel)

|Says
|what exists at the end
|how the work gets done on the way there

|Example
|"members can be enrolled in courses online"
|"the team meets weekly and keeps decisions in the repository"

|Checked
|at the acceptance, on the product
|during the project, on the way of working

|If it is missing
|nobody knows what was promised
|the result may be right and nobody can repeat it
|===

Both belong in the charter, marked as what they are. The frequent mistake is a
charter full of process goals — meetings, tools, methods — with no statement
of what will exist at the end.

=== Operationalising goals: the goal hierarchy

An overall goal such as "trainers spend less time on administration" is true,
agreed — and impossible to act on or check. *Operationalising* it
(Operationalisierung) means breaking it down until every part can be acted on and
checked. The tool for that is the *goal hierarchy* (Zielhierarchie).

It starts from a root, the *overall goal* (Gesamtziel). Below it sit goal
classes — technical, economic, ecological, … — and below those the sub-goals,
refined over one or more levels until each one is described by a *criterion*
(Kriterium) and an *extent* (Ausmaß). A criterion is a property of the solution;
the extent says how far it has to be realised for the goal to count as reached.

Only the bottom row can be implemented and checked at the acceptance. The rows
above explain *why* it is there — and a box at the bottom that cannot be traced
up to the root is a wish that slipped in.

The hierarchy can be built in three ways:

[cols="1,3",options="header"]
|===
|Approach |How it works

|*Top-down* (Top-down-Ansatz)
|start from the overall goal and decompose and refine it, level by level, down to
 sub-goals with criterion and extent

|*Bottom-up* (Bottom-up-Ansatz)
|detailed goals already exist — wishes from the interviews, requirements of the
 club. They are grouped by their contribution to the overall goal and assigned
 to goal classes

|*Mixed approach* (gemischter Ansatz)
|neither pure form works best, so the hierarchy is built iteratively, using both
 directions in turn
|===

Top-down alone produces sub-goals nobody checked against what is possible;
bottom-up alone produces a pile of details without a common direction. A school
project is mixed in practice: the client brings the overall goal, the
elicitation brings the details, and the charter is where both meet.

=== The four questions of a goal agreement

Before a goal is written down it should survive four questions. They come from
the Coverdale method and they are the fastest test there is.

[cols="1,3",options="header"]
|===
|Question |What it forces out

|*WHAT FOR?* (Wozu?)
|the purpose behind the goal. If the answer is "because it was asked for", the
 goal is not understood yet

|*FOR WHOM?* (Für wen?)
|the stakeholder who benefits. A goal without a named beneficiary is usually
 somebody's hobby

|*END RESULT?* (Endergebnis?)
|what concretely exists when it is reached — a thing, not an activity

|*SUCCESS CRITERIA?* (Erfolgskriterien?)
|how a third party recognises that it was reached
|===

Question four is the one that turns a wish into a goal, and questions one and
two are the ones that occasionally delete a goal entirely — which is a good
outcome, and much cheaper now than in May.

=== How goals relate to each other

Goals in the same charter are not independent, and the relationship has a name.

[cols="1,3",options="header"]
|===
|Relationship |Meaning

|*Conflicting goals* (Zielkonkurrenz)
|reaching one makes the other harder — more functions against an earlier
 deadline. Must be decided, not averaged away

|*Complementary goals* (Zielkomplementarität)
|reaching one helps the other — a clean data model helps both the export and
 the deletion requirement

|*Independent goals* (Zielneutralität)
|the two do not influence each other. Rarer than it looks
|===

Conflicting goals are the interesting case: it is not a planning error, it is the
normal state, and the charter's job is to say which of the two wins when they
collide.

=== Sub-goals and their weight

The *sub-goals* (Teilziele) of the hierarchy are not equally important —
pretending that they are is how a project ends up with a perfect export function
and no working enrolment.

Weighting them is a small weighted scoring (Nutzwertanalyse): give each sub-goal a weight, state
what the weight is based on, and the decision "what do we build first" becomes a
calculation rather than a mood. The method itself is taught in year 4, in
`governance-weighted-scoring`.

== Non-goals

Non-goals (Nicht-Ziele) are what the project explicitly does not do. They cost one line each
and prevent the two most expensive conversations of the year: scope creep during
the project, and disappointment at the acceptance.

----
Non-goals:
  - no mobile app, browser only
  - no payment handling
  - no import from the old Excel sheets
----

A non-goal is not a promise never to do it. It is a statement that it is out of
scope for *this* project, in *this* year.

== Decisions

* Every project charter names its stakeholders, marked as internal or external,
  with the interest each of them pursues.
* Goals are written so a third party can check them; unverifiable goals go back
  to the team for rewriting.
* Every goal states scope and quality, schedule and cost, and is marked as
  outcome goal or process goal.
* Competing goals are named as competing, with the decision which one wins.
* Every charter has a non-goals section. An empty one is not accepted — if
  nothing is out of scope, the scope was not thought through.

== Pitfalls

* Only listing users and forgetting whoever has to operate or approve the thing.
* Listing stakeholders without their interest. The name alone tells you nothing
  about what they will do at the acceptance.
* Goals that measure your effort instead of the result ("we implement a REST
  API"). Nobody outside your team cares how it is built; they care what it does.
* A charter of pure process goals: much method, no promised result.
* Goals written after the fact, matched to what was finished. That is what the
  frozen charter exists to prevent.
* Non-goals that are secretly plans ("no mobile app *for now*"). Then it is not a
  non-goal.
