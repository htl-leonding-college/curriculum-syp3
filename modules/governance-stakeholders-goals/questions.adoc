== Who counts as a stakeholder, and which four types should you look for?

covers: lo-1

.Answer
[%collapsible]
====
A stakeholder is *anyone affected by the project or able to affect it*. Note both
halves — people who cannot influence the project still count, and people with no
interest in it still count if they hold a veto.

[cols="1,2,2",options="header"]
|===
|Type |Example in a school project |What they can do to you

|User
|the trainer who records attendance
|refuse to use it if it is slower than paper

|Client
|the club, the teacher, the school
|change what "finished" means

|Operator
|whoever runs the server afterwards
|refuse to host it

|Affected party
|members whose data is stored
|data protection, and rightly so
|===

Missing one is the most common way a finished project turns out to be unusable —
and it is almost always the operator or the affected party, because neither of
them appears in the conversation where the idea is born.

Sorted by *interest* and *influence*, the four quadrants suggest four different
treatments: involve closely, keep satisfied, keep informed, inform.

Points the answer must contain:

* Anyone affected by the project or able to affect it
* Users, client, operators, affected parties (for example data subjects)
* The dangerous quadrant is high influence with low interest
====

== Turn "the app should be fast" into a verifiable goal

covers: lo-2

.Answer
[%collapsible]
====
"Opening the attendance list for a group of 30 takes under one second on the lab
laptops."

Three ingredients make the difference:

* an *observable action* — opening the attendance list,
* a *measurable condition* — under one second,
* a *context in which it holds* — a group of 30, on the lab laptops.

The test is not whether the sentence sounds professional. It is whether *somebody
who was not involved can check it without asking you what you meant*. "Fast"
fails that test: fast compared with what, on which machine, with how much data?
Two people can both be honest and disagree about whether it was met, which means
the goal cannot settle anything at the acceptance.

[cols="1,1",options="header"]
|===
|Wish |Verifiable goal

|"Users should like it."
|"A trainer can record a full session without written instructions, in under two
 minutes."

|"The data must be safe."
|"Personal data is stored only for members with a signed consent, and can be
 deleted on request within one working day."
|===

Wishes are allowed to exist — write them down as wishes. What is not allowed is
calling one a goal, because then the project has a yardstick that cannot measure.

Points the answer must contain:

* Observable action, measurable condition, context
* For example: attendance list for 30 members opens in under one second on the
  lab laptops
* A third party must be able to check it without asking what you meant
====

== Why does a charter contain non-goals?

covers: lo-3

.Answer
[%collapsible]
====
Because they cost one line each and prevent the two most expensive conversations
of the year.

----
Non-goals:
  - no mobile app, browser only
  - no payment handling
  - no import from the old Excel sheets
----

The first conversation is *scope creep during the project*: in March somebody
asks "could it also do payments?", and without a non-goal there is no cheap way
to say no — the discussion starts from scratch and usually ends with a yes that
nobody had time for. With the line written down, the answer is "that is out of
scope; if we want it, we change the charter deliberately".

The second is *disappointment at the acceptance*: the client expected the Excel
import because nobody ever said it was not coming. A non-goal is the cheapest
form of expectation management there is.

A non-goal is not a promise never to do the thing. It says: out of scope for
*this* project, in *this* year. And it must not be a secret plan — "no mobile app
*for now*" is a roadmap entry, not a non-goal, and it will be read as a promise.

An empty non-goals section is not accepted in this course: if nothing is out of
scope, the scope was not thought through.

Points the answer must contain:

* They prevent scope creep during the project and disappointment at acceptance
* One line each, cheap
* A non-goal is not a permanent refusal, it is out of scope for this project
====

== Why is "we implement a REST API" a bad goal?

covers: lo-2, lo-3

.Answer
[%collapsible]
====
Because it measures *your effort* instead of the *result*. It is satisfied the
moment the API exists, whether or not anybody can do anything useful with it —
and it would still be "achieved" if the trainer never manages to record a single
session.

Nobody outside the team cares how the thing is built. The client cares what
somebody can do afterwards that they could not do before; the API is a means, and
means belong in the design, not in the goals.

There is a second, quieter problem: a goal phrased as an implementation decision
*forbids every other implementation*. If halfway through it turns out that a
server-rendered page would do the job in a third of the time, the charter now
says no — for no reason anybody would defend if asked.

Rewritten: "A trainer can record attendance for a session from the phone at the
door and see it in the club database within a minute." That says what changes for
a person, is checkable by somebody who was not there, and leaves the technical
route open.

Points the answer must contain:

* It measures effort, not result
* Nobody outside the team cares how it is built
* Rewrite it as what a user can then do
====

== What is the risk in the high-influence, low-interest quadrant?

covers: lo-1

.Answer
[%collapsible]
====
They are not paying attention — until the moment they say no.

The typical occupants are the school's IT administration, the data protection
officer, the person who owns the server. None of them is interested in your
project week by week; each of them can stop it in a single sentence: "that may
not be hosted here", "personal data may not be processed like that".

The danger is not the veto itself but *when* it arrives. Because they are not
watching, the objection surfaces at the point where they are finally asked —
usually at the deployment, in June, when changing the answer costs a rebuild
instead of a decision.

The treatment is therefore *keep satisfied*: ask early, in writing, while the veto
is still cheap. Five minutes with the administrator in October settles what a
redesign in June cannot. The other quadrants are less demanding — involve the
high-influence, high-interest people closely; keep the interested-but-powerless
informed, because they are your best source of requirements; inform the rest.

Points the answer must contain:

* They are not watching until they say no
* Keep them satisfied and informed early, when a veto is still cheap
====
